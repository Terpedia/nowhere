Psychoactivity & absinthe

Ethanol / thujone / myth

Absinthe is genuinely psychoactive because it contains alcohol. The harder question is whether its botanicals make it psychedelic. Current evidence points toward CNS activity and toxicological risk—not a classic psychedelic effect.

The short answer

Psychoactive is not the same as psychedelic

“Psychoactive” means capable of affecting the brain or behavior. By that broad definition, absinthe is psychoactive primarily through ethanol. “Psychedelic” usually refers to a distinct class of drugs whose main effects involve serotonergic 5-HT2A signaling, altered perception, and hallucination. No ordinary absinthe ingredient has been established to produce that effect at normal beverage concentrations.

Pathway analysis

Why the absinthe drunk may feel different

The best-supported model is not “absinthe contains a hidden psychedelic.” It is a simultaneous interaction between ethanol’s broad CNS depression and thujone’s opposing GABAergic excitation. The subjective difference is a testable hypothesis, not a proven single pathway in humans.

Absinthe serving Ethanol + variable α-/β-thujone Observed composition varies by product and dose.
→
GABA-A modulation Thujone reduces GABAergic inhibitory currents in receptor studies. Direct in-vitro pharmacology.
→
Net CNS balance Ethanol pushes inhibition; thujone may push excitation. Mechanistically plausible; dose and exposure matter.
→
Different subjective profile Less purely sedating, more alert, tense, or disinhibited. Human study supports attention/mood changes, not psychedelia.

The critical boundary: receptor antagonism can explain excitation, anxiety, impaired attention, or seizure risk; it does not by itself explain visual hallucinations or establish a psychedelic mechanism.

Clearance branch

Thujone is also metabolized

α-Thujone Exact chemical identity; α and β are distinct diastereomers.
→
CYP2A6 oxidation Major 4- and 7-hydroxylation in human liver preparations; CYP3A4 and CYP2B6 contribute. Direct in-vitro human-liver evidence.
→
Hydroxythujones / conjugates Human urine studies detect 4- and 7-hydroxythujone after conjugate release. Clearance is supported; contribution to the “different drunk” is unestablished.

This is a metabolism pathway, not proof of a psychoactive metabolite. The available work supports biotransformation and detoxification, while the human dose–concentration–effect relationship remains uncertain.

The historical reputation

Why absinthe became “psychedelic” in popular memory

The old reputation likely combines very high alcohol intake, chronic heavy use, sleep loss, malnutrition, withdrawal, variable or adulterated products, and the mythology of nineteenth-century bohemia. Historical claims of extremely high thujone concentrations have not been reliably confirmed in modern analyses.

A controlled human study found that a high-thujone drink impaired attention and partly counteracted alcohol’s anxiolytic effect. It did not demonstrate hallucinations or a psychedelic state.

The important distinction

More likely toxic than psychedelic

Concentrated wormwood, hyssop, fennel, rosemary, sage, or other essential oils can contain convulsant monoterpene ketones. That is a safety concern, not a route to a controlled or desirable “trip.” Essential oils should not be added to drinks as a way to intensify psychoactivity.

For Nowhere

Measure the batch, don’t mythologize it

The useful analytical panel is GC-MS for ethanol, α-/β-thujone, trans-anethole, fenchone, camphor, pinocamphone, isopinocamphone, and related volatiles. Sensory claims should be separated from toxicology, and every result should be interpreted alongside ABV and serving size.
